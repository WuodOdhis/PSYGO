# Your Brain Probably Isn't Making the DMT You Think It Is

*The "deathbed DMT" story is repeated with total confidence. The evidence behind it is thinner than the sentence.*

---

You have heard the claim. Maybe at a party, maybe in a documentary, maybe from a friend who read one book and now explains it to everyone.

When you die, your pineal gland releases DMT into your brain. That is the white light. That is the tunnel. That is why near-death experiences feel the way they do.

It is a beautiful idea. It is also stated far more confidently than the evidence supports.

This is not a debunking. DMT is real, and mammals do make it. Trace amounts are detectable in tissue. But "your brain makes DMT" and "your brain makes enough DMT to send you somewhere" are two different claims. The first is well supported. The second is the one everyone repeats, and it is the one nobody has demonstrated in humans.

Here is what we actually know, and where the story gets ahead of the science.

---

## First, what DMT is

DMT — dimethyltryptamine — is a psychedelic. It is the molecule in ayahuasca, and it is one of the most potent psychedelics we know of. Taken at a high enough dose, it produces intense, brief, and often overwhelming experiences.

It also occurs in nature. Many plants contain it. So do many animals, humans included. Your body can detect it in trace amounts, and your body can make it.

That part is not controversial. The controversy is about quantity, and about what quantity is enough to do.

---

## One question is actually four

"Is endogenous DMT real?" sounds like a single question. It is at least four, and each can be answered separately:

1. **Detection.** Can we measure DMT in human tissue at all?
2. **Biosynthesis.** Does human tissue make it, and through which enzyme?
3. **Function.** Does it do anything — signalling, immunity, development?
4. **Sufficiency.** Do the amounts in your brain ever get high enough to produce the effects of a psychedelic dose?

Only the fourth supports the deathbed story. And the fourth is the one with the least human evidence, because it requires something nobody has done: measuring DMT in human brain tissue at the moment it would matter.

The field's own review articles say this plainly. Writing in *Frontiers in Neuroscience*, Steven Barker, Ede Frecska and colleagues note that data on actual DMT levels in the human brain "simply does not exist." David Nichols, in a 2018 review in the *Journal of Psychopharmacology*, puts it more directly: very minute amounts of DMT have been detected in brain tissue, but they are not enough to produce psychoactive effects.

That does not mean the question is closed. It means the confident version of the story is running ahead of the measurements.

---

## The study everyone cites

Ask for the evidence and someone will point to a 2019 paper in *Scientific Reports*, by a team including Jon Dean and Steven Barker. It is genuinely interesting work, and it is usually described in one line: scientists found DMT in rat brains at levels comparable to serotonin.

That line is accurate and incomplete. Here is what the study actually did.

They used a technique called microdialysis, which samples the fluid around living cells, to measure DMT in the cortex of awake rats. They found levels in the same range as serotonin and dopamine, two neurotransmitters everyone agrees do important work.

Then two details get left out of the retelling.

First, they removed the pineal glands from some rats, and the DMT was still there. If the pineal is the source, that should not happen.

Second, during induced cardiac arrest, DMT levels in the visual cortex spiked. That is the detail that fuels the deathbed story — and it is the detail that needs the most care.

---

## Concentration is not experience

Here is the crucial distinction, and it is where the popular version quietly breaks.

Finding a molecule is not the same as finding an effect. Your brain is full of molecules at concentrations that do nothing in particular. What matters is whether a concentration is high enough to activate the receptors that produce a psychedelic experience.

During cardiac arrest, the brain releases a storm of neurotransmitters. Serotonin is one of them. And serotonin, on its own, can activate the receptor that produces psychedelic-like effects in animals. So finding DMT inside that storm does not prove DMT caused anything. It might just be one more chemical in a flood.

A year later, Charles Nichols and David Nichols made exactly this argument in the *ALIUS Bulletin*. Their point was not that the measurements were wrong. It was that concentration is not effect.

The authors of the original study agreed with the caution. Their own paper says it is unknown whether the DMT levels at cardiac arrest could produce the effects of an exogenous dose. In plain terms: we found it, we do not know if it does anything.

---

## The pineal is smaller than the story

There is a second problem, and it is simple arithmetic.

The adult human pineal gland weighs less than 0.2 grams. It produces roughly 30 micrograms of melatonin a day, which is its main known job. Nichols has walked through the numbers and concluded the gland cannot produce physiologically meaningful amounts of DMT.

Then, in 2023, a study found that INMT — the enzyme long assumed to be responsible for making DMT — is not actually required for DMT production in rats. That cuts both ways. It weakens the "pineal pump" model, and it means we do not yet know where the DMT we can measure comes from.

None of this makes the question boring. It makes it open.

---

## Why the myth travels faster

The four-question collapse is not a failure of intelligence. It is a failure of storytelling economics.

"The brain makes DMT" is true, checkable, and astonishing. "Trace amounts of a tryptamine are detectable in rodent cortex and may or may not do anything" is also true, and nobody shares it.

So the story compresses. Presence becomes purpose. Purpose becomes experience. Each step is a small, forgivable shortcut, and together they turn a careful literature into a slogan.

The same compression happens across psychedelic science, and it has a cost. Hype borrows credibility from real research, then leaves the field holding the bill — in public trust, in regulation, in funding decisions made by people who were promised more than the data delivers.

---

## What would actually settle it

Not another rat study. Three things:

- **Human tissue measurements.** Quantify DMT in human brain tissue, region by region, with controls for MAO — the enzyme that destroys DMT so quickly that every measurement is a race against the clock.
- **Receptor modelling.** Work out what concentration actually occupies 5-HT2A, the receptor behind the psychedelic effect, at a level consistent with subjective experience.
- **A claim that can fail.** Right now the deathbed story is compatible with every result. A theory that cannot be wrong cannot be tested, and cannot be trusted.

## Why this matters for PsyDAO

PsyDAO exists on a simple bet: that a community can fund serious psychedelic research, and that the research should be legible to the community that funds it.

That bet only pays off if someone does the translation.

Most writing about this field is aimed at two audiences: specialists who already know the vocabulary, and believers who already accept the conclusion. The curious newcomer — the person who might fund a grant, join a working group, or simply stop repeating a myth — is usually left out.

That is the reader I write for. Not a dumbed-down reader. A reader who is intelligent, busy, and unwilling to be talked down to. Someone who deserves to know the difference between "your brain makes DMT" and "your brain makes enough DMT to matter," because that difference is the whole story.

If PsyDAO's research is going to be funded by regular people, it has to be readable by regular people. That is not decoration on top of the science. It is infrastructure underneath it.

---

## The better question

The interesting question is not whether your brain makes DMT. It does, in trace amounts, as it makes many things.

The interesting question is whether a molecule this pharmacologically loud is ever allowed, by the body's own controls, to be loud enough to matter.

That question is still open. It deserves writing that does not close it early.
